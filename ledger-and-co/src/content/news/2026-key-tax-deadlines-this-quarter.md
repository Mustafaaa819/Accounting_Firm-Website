Every firm publishes a deadline calendar in July and most of them are useless,
because they list every date in the tax year without saying which ones carry a
penalty, which ones carry interest, and which ones are only a deadline in the
sense that a form exists.

This is the shorter version. Eight dates between now and the end of January,
what happens if you miss each, and where we would actually spend your
attention.

## July

**6 July — P11D and P11D(b).** Benefits in kind for the year to 5 April, for
every benefit you have not payrolled. The penalty is £100 per fifty employees
per month, which sounds small and compounds badly on a file everyone has
forgotten. Hard deadline.

**22 July — Class 1A National Insurance.** The employer contribution on those
same benefits, where you pay electronically. The 19th if you still pay by
cheque, which almost nobody should. Interest runs from the day after; there is
no penalty until much later, so this is a date where a few days' slip costs
you pounds rather than hundreds. Still, pay it with the P11D — splitting the
two is how it gets lost.

**31 July — second payment on account.** Half your prior year's income tax
liability, due whether or not this year is going worse than last. This is the
one that surprises people, and it is also the one with a genuine escape hatch:
if your income has genuinely fallen, you can apply to reduce the payments on
account rather than paying and reclaiming in January.

> Reducing a payment on account is not a judgement call you should make
> optimistically. Reduce it below what you eventually owe and HMRC charges
> interest back to the original due date. Reduce it deliberately, on figures,
> or not at all.

## Autumn

**5 October — notifying chargeability.** If you had income in the year to
5 April 2026 that HMRC does not already know about — a first year of
self-employment, a property let, a capital gain, a business you started in
March and told nobody about — this is the date by which you must register. The
penalty is behaviour-based and geared to the tax at stake, which means it can
be far more than the £100 people expect.

This is the date most likely to apply to someone reading this who does not
think it applies to them.

**31 October — paper self assessment returns.** Only relevant if you file on
paper. If you do, the practical advice is to stop; the online deadline is
three months later for the same return.

## Winter

**30 December — the coding-out election.** File your return online by this
date and, if you owe less than £3,000 and have PAYE income to collect it
from, HMRC will spread the liability across next year's tax code instead of
taking it in one payment on 31 January. It is an interest-free instalment plan
that costs nothing but filing early, and it is the single most under-used date
on this list.

**31 January — the one everybody knows.** Online return, the balancing payment
for 2025/26, and the first payment on account for 2026/27, all on the same
day. £100 immediately if the return is late even by a day and even if you owe
nothing. Then daily penalties from three months. On the payment side: 5% of
the tax unpaid at thirty days, again at six months, again at twelve, with
interest throughout.

## The company dates, which do not fit a calendar

Corporation tax and Companies House run on your own year end rather than the
tax year, which is why they get left off lists like this one and then missed.
For a company with a 31 December year end:

- **1 October** — corporation tax payment, nine months and a day after the
  period end. Note that the payment is due three months *before* the return.
- **31 December** — statutory accounts to Companies House, nine months after
  the period end. The late filing penalty is automatic, doubles if you were
  late the year before, and is not negotiable.
- **31 December** — the CT600 itself, twelve months after the period end.

Shift those by the difference between your year end and the calendar year.
